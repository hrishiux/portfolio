Air–Fuel Mixture and E20 Petrol in Three Vehicles

A study of air–fuel mixture in the three vehicles I own: a turbocharged Octavia Mk2 1.8 TSI, a Suzuki SY416 and a Honda CBR250R. It covers how each engine controls its mixture, the public evidence and logs from closely related engines, and the effect of India's move to E20 petrol. 01

Stoichiometry and lambda

Air-fuel ratio, or AFR, is the mass of air an engine burns for each unit mass of fuel. Petrol burns completely at about 14.7 kilograms of air per kilogram of fuel, the stoichiometric point. Ethanol carries oxygen in its own molecule, so it needs less air, about 9.0.

Lambda (λ) divides the actual ratio by the stoichiometric one. At λ 1.00 there is exactly enough air for the fuel, whatever the fuel is. Below 1 the mixture is rich, above 1 it is lean. Engine computers and tuners work in lambda for this reason.

A wideband sensor measures oxygen, and most gauges report it on a petrol scale by default, so a gauge reading of 14.7 means λ 1.00 on any fuel. On E20 that same reading is about 13.5 kilograms of air per kilogram of fuel. The ruler below shows where engines are usually aimed, with the AFR scale redrawn for the fuel you pick.

The three vehicles

My three vehicles cover the main ways a petrol engine gets its air and fuel: a turbocharger with fuel sprayed straight into the cylinder, a non-turbo engine with an injector in each intake port, and a single cylinder that revs past 10,000 rpm. They are parked in Palakkad and I am in Bengaluru, so this first version is built from public data on the same engines and, where those run out, their closest relatives.

The CBR is the only one of my three with a measured full-throttle mixture: about 14 : 1 on the stock ECU, λ 0.95, leaner than the 0.85 to 0.92 band tuners aim for on non-turbo engines. A replacement ECU took it to about 12 : 1, added 0.53 hp at the peak and moved the rev limit from 10,500 to 12,300 rpm.

Audi's training manual for the EA888 says the mixture is set to λ 1 in all operating ranges except just after a start. Audi's 2011 text on the third generation says its water-cooled exhaust manifold removes the need for a richer mixture at full load. Two owners of the 2.0-litre from the same second-generation family report full-throttle readings: λ 0.82 on a stock fuel map with a bigger turbo, and AFR 13.4 falling to 11.7 at 6,000 rpm on a stage 2 file. No full log was posted, so the next section goes to the closest relatives that were.

04

Logs from closely related engines

Where my own engines had no public logs, I went one step out: to the 1.8-litre turbo the EA888 replaced and to the EA888's third generation, to a G16B sibling, and to non-turbo engines built the way the G16B is, a VW 1.6 here and a Honda 1.8 in the E20 section. Every chart names exactly which engine it shows. They show how engines of each kind behave; they are not readings from my vehicles.

On its stock map the 1.8T held λ 0.87 to 0.91 from 3,800 to 6,300 rpm, then dropped to 0.79 near 6,700. The chip took it to 0.75 by 6,200. The non-turbo 1.6 asked for 0.90 from 2,000 to 5,200 rpm and 0.75 to 0.76 above 5,500, where its sensor read 0.80.

The third-generation EA888 is the one with public logs. On the factory calibration this 2.0 read λ 0.98 to 1.02 from 2,000 to 4,300 rpm at full throttle. That is the set point EcuTek's tuning notes give for the third generation, "a lambda set point of 1 is targeted for the entire operating range", and Audi's 2011 text says its water-cooled exhaust manifold removes the need for a richer mixture at full load. Above 5,000 rpm it eased to 0.92 to 0.96. EcuTek lists what can move the set point richer: high intake or catalyst temperature and turbo protection. This log has no exhaust-temperature channel, so it does not show which one acted.

The Stage 1 file raised charge pressure from 2.2 to 2.9 bar absolute and ran 0.80 to 0.86 from 2,050 rpm to the limiter, inside the usual turbo target. A 1.8 TFSI of the same generation on a Stage 1 file from the same tuner ran 0.80 to 0.85.

The modelled exhaust temperature crossed 920 °C at about 5,200 rpm, and the enrichment factor climbed to 15 to 17%. Lambda fell from 0.875 to 0.75 between 5,500 and 5,900 rpm. A forum member describes the stock 1.8T as running λ 0.953 until this protection steps in, and MRC Tuning put it this way: in stock mode the computer runs as lean as it can without hitting the limit.

Right after the start the computer asked for λ 0.85 and eased up to 1.00 over about 20 seconds. Most of the drive then sits on 1.00 with the sensor wandering a few hundredths either side, which is closed-loop control at work. Both full-throttle pulls step down to the requested 0.90. Lift-offs pin the reading at 1.20, mostly with the logged injection time at zero: the injectors shut off on the overrun.

A narrowband sensor can only tell rich from lean. The tuner who posted this trace describes it as sitting near 0 volts when lean and around 0.8 volts when rich, and puts the cycle at about once a second. The computer keeps nudging fuel back across the line, which is how an engine with this kind of sensor holds λ 1 on average. My SY416 uses the same kind: a narrowband sensor I replaced myself.

India's move to E20

India blended 1.53% ethanol into its petrol in 2013-14. By the 2025-26 supply year the average reached 20%, and from April 2026 all petrol sold is E20 with a minimum research octane number of 95.

Effect of E20 on fuelling

The US Department of Energy's test E20 fuels carried 6.8% to 7.2% oxygen by mass. For the same mass of fuel, that oxygen leans the mixture. At light load an engine computer holding λ 1 sees this through its oxygen sensor and adds fuel. At full throttle it typically switches to an open-loop enrichment map. The US researchers' explanation for what happens next is whether the computer carries its learned correction into that map.

On petrol the Civic's full-throttle samples bunch around λ 0.81, and the report puts the average at about 0.82. On E20 the bunch moves to about 0.87: still rich, about 6% leaner. The report says lambda typically runs 6% to 8% leaner on E20 when an engine does not apply its learned fuel trim at full throttle. It classed 19 of its 27 models that way, this Civic and a 2009 Corolla 1.8 among them.

Fuel economy on E20

Every source agrees E20 gives fewer kilometres per litre. They disagree on how many, partly because they compare it against different fuels. Calculated from the US lab's measured fuels, a litre of E20 holds 6.8% to 7.0% less energy than a litre of E0, and 3.7% to 3.9% less than a litre of E10.

Sources and method

Method

  • Units. Wideband AFR readings are on the petrol scale, so λ = AFR ÷ 14.7.
  • Stoichiometric blends. AFR = w × 9.0 + (1 − w) × 14.7, with w the ethanol mass fraction from densities of 0.789 (ethanol) and 0.746 (petrol). At 10% ethanol by volume it returns 14.10 against a published 14.1. At 20% it returns 13.51.
  • Energy per litre. Lower heating value × specific gravity for each of the twelve US DOE test fuels, indexed to the same lab's E0.
  • Digitising. The SY416 curve was read off the 800 × 562 px chart by colour, calibrated on its gridlines (115 px per 1,000 rpm; 3.94 px per unit). Peaks came out at 106.6 N·m and 71.1 hp against 106.79 and 71.24 printed on the chart. The entry is labelled only "Maruti Suzuki Baleno"; it was posted in September 2015 and quotes 94 bhp and 130 N·m, figures that fit the 1.6-litre SY416.
  • Reading a PDF figure. The Honda Civic histogram is drawn as vector lines in the ORNL report, so its points were read from the PDF's drawing commands and scaled with the figure's own axis ticks (0.70 to 1.10; 0 to 3,500). The samples below 0.95 average 0.822 on E0 and 0.876 on E20; the report's text says about 0.82 and 0.87.
  • Third-generation logs. Rows are the VCDS samples inside each full-throttle pull, about 9 per second, as posted. Charge pressure is absolute, so about 1 bar is atmosphere.
  • Conflicting figures. For 2020-21 and 2022-23 the blend chart uses the 2025 Rajya Sabha series (8.5% and 12%); the 2024 Lok Sabha reply gives 8.17% and 12.06%.

Limitations

  • None of the measurements come from my own vehicles. They come from the same models or, where marked, from close relatives.
  • No full lambda log turned up for the EA888 Gen 1 or Gen 2, only two Gen 2 owners' stated readings, and nothing for the G16B. The curves come from the 1.8T 20-valve that preceded the EA888, the EA888's third generation, a G13B sibling, a VW 1.6 non-turbo and a Honda 1.8 non-turbo. A relative shows how that kind of engine behaves; it can differ from mine in calibration, sensors and hardware.
  • The third-generation logs were posted by a tuner without notes. The file names say "stock" and "STG1"; the car's model and mileage are not given. No readable fourth-generation (EA888 evo4) log turned up.
  • The Miata.net and FitFreak forums sit behind a bot check, so they were not read.
  • Several of the largest owner forums, including VWVortex, CBR250.net and TTForum, restrict automated access, so they were not searched for this version.
  • The 2012 CBR250R dyno charts are no longer online. The figures used are the magazine's own summary of them, "about 14 : 1" and "about 12 : 1".
  • Pump-fuel readings are a tuning shop's test-bottle measurements, not laboratory analysis.
  • The US Department of Energy cars are 1999–2007 American models. None of them is one of mine.
  • The E20 source for April 2026 onward is a summary of the PIB release, not the release itself.
