Runs your fleet. Never without asking.

An agentic control plane for Linux servers. You describe the outcome. It plans the work, holds every change behind a gate you own, and can undo all of it in one action.

The work is boring.
The risk is not.

Fleets drift

Kernels, packages, configs, and the one-off fix someone applied at 2am and never wrote down. Every node is a slightly different machine by month six.

Patching is mechanical

Deciding whether tonight is a safe night to patch is not. That judgment is the entire job, and it sits in one tired person's head.

So the work waits

It waits for the human with forty other tabs open, until a CVE deadline or an outage decides the schedule instead.

in policy drifted unreachable

Three things sit between a sentence and root.

The intelligence is the cheap part. What makes autonomy survivable is everything wrapped around it.

intent

A sentence becomes a named plan

Plain English is resolved against live inventory into a plan with an explicit blast radius: which nodes, in what order, what reboots, what it expects to be true afterward.

policy

You decide what it may do alone

Rules you write in version control sort every action into three buckets: runs unattended, waits for a person, never runs. The agent proposes. Policy is what actually consents.

transaction

Every change lands on a snapshot

Nothing is applied in place. Changes go to a new boot slot or a filesystem snapshot, health is checked, and a failed check reverts the node without anyone being paged.

One night, one change, one thing going wrong.

A kernel upgrade across 240 nodes, starting at 01:12. You will be asked to approve once. Something fails on purpose.

It is not a new operating system.

Nobody reinstalls a production fleet for a startup. Keel runs as a daemon beside the distro you already certified, and leans on what that OS already gives you: snapshots, transactional updates, signed packages.

Your workloads Containers, databases, whatever was already running. Untouched.
Keel Intent, policy, transactions, ledger. One daemon per node, one control plane per fleet.
Debian · RHEL · Ubuntu · SUSE Your distro, your support contract, your compliance certifications. All still valid.

What this demo does not answer yet.

Who holds the pager when the agent is wrong?

Auto-revert covers the failures a health check can see. It does nothing for the quiet ones, where every check passes and something is subtly broken for nine hours. Accountability has to be designed, and it cannot be designed away.

What does policy look like to someone who has never written policy?

The whole model rests on rules that a team writes honestly. If authoring them feels like writing firewall config in 2009, people will approve everything by default and the gate becomes decoration.

How does trust get earned?

Probably one action class at a time. Log rotation before kernels. Read-only before write. The product may be less about capability and more about a ladder someone climbs over months.
